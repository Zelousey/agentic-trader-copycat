What it's actually looking for

Four shapes on a chart. Nothing else counts.

Every candidate gets sorted into one of these — or thrown out. If a stock's chart doesn't look like one of these, it doesn't matter how exciting the ticker is.

Pullback in an uptrend

Climbing steadily, pauses, catches its breath near support.

Bull flag

A sharp run, then a tight, orderly drift sideways — not a collapse.

Breakout + retest

Breaks a level, comes back to prove that level now holds.

Higher-low reversal

A decline that stops short of its last low, then turns back up.

How it decides

Four checks, every session, before anything gets suggested.

01

Read the market

Checks whether stocks broadly are trending up, down, or sideways first — the bar for a good setup gets stricter when the backdrop is weak.

02

Scan for pullbacks, not runners

Screens hundreds of stocks for ones climbing steadily that have recently paused — then explicitly throws out anything that's already made its big move. Chasing is the most common way this kind of trade goes wrong.

03

Score it, don't just spot it

Trend, support, volume, reward-to-risk, relative strength — scored out of 80. Below the bar, it gets dropped, even if it "looks" interesting.

04

You decide, every time

If something clears the bar, you get the entry, stop, target, and exact position size — sized against a fixed capital cap, never a guess — with Accept and Decline right there. Tap Accept and the trade goes in. Tap Decline, or don't respond, and it's skipped. Nothing is ever bought without your say-so on that specific trade.

Why "Zelos"

It's not envy. It's knowing you're capable.

“Emulation is pain caused by seeing the presence, in persons whose nature is like our own, of good things that are highly valued and are possible for ourselves to acquire; but it is felt not because others have these goods, but because we have not got them ourselves.” — Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book II, Ch. 11 (on ζῆλος / zelos, “emulation”)

Aristotle drew a sharp line between two feelings that look similar from the outside. Envy wants the other person to lose what they have. Zelos — emulation — wants what a capable peer has because you can see, clearly, that it's within reach for you too. He called it a feeling good people have.

That's the distinction this is named after. Not chasing a stock because someone else is up on it — that's the trade this strategy explicitly refuses to make. Going after the same kind of opportunity, on your own terms, when the setup is actually there for you. The pain isn't watching someone else win. It's sitting on the sidelines when the door was genuinely open.

This isn't personalized advice; think about what account size makes sense for you before connecting one.
